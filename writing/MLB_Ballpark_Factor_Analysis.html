MLB Ballpark Factor Analysis

Report By: Bryce Gelarden
April 2026

Research Question

Do ballpark environments in Major League Baseball explain variance in pitch type frequencies and pitch type contacy quality across the 2023, 2024, and 2025 seasons in a post pitch-clock and defensive-shift era?

Sample ~175,000 total swings (18,000 swings per park)
Seasons 2023, 2024, 2025
Ballparks 29 MLB stadiums

Notebooks

01 · Data Pipeline

This notebook establishes the data foundation for the study by importing the raw baseball event records, standardizing field names, and aligning pitch, swing, and ballpark metadata into a single analytical structure. The objective is to ensure each observation is tied to a consistent season, stadium, batter, pitcher, pitch type, and swing outcome before any interpretation is attempted.

The pipeline also screens for incomplete records, removes unusable observations, and creates a final sample that is balanced enough to support valid comparisons across parks and seasons. This step matters because ballpark effects are only interpretable when the underlying dataset is structurally consistent and free from obvious sample distortions.

Data Cleaning / Sample Construction

02 · Exploratory Analytics

This notebook provides the first descriptive view of the data and identifies the major patterns in pitch usage and swing behavior before formal modeling. It is designed to answer simple but essential questions: which pitch types dominate, how often contact occurs, how outcomes vary by venue, and whether the seasonal sample looks stable enough for park-level inference.

The exploration shows the distribution of pitch types, swing outcomes, and contact events at a broad level, and it highlights the first signs that parks may not all operate under the same offensive environment. In other words, the notebook establishes that the data contain genuine variation beyond random league-wide noise, which motivates the more formal park-adjustment work.

  • Pitch type frequency distributions across seasons
  • Basic swing-result summaries by ballpark and season

Exploratory Charts / Summary Diagnostics

03 · SOV Construction

The stadium-adjusted outcome value (SOV) is the central analytical construct of the project. This notebook converts raw event data into park-relative measures that isolate venue-specific effects from broader league trends, seasonal shifts, and general pitch-and-swing patterns.

The logic is to compare each ballpark against a baseline derived from the broader sample, then examine whether a park systematically inflates or suppresses a given outcome after accounting for the wider distribution. This makes it possible to distinguish between a true park effect and a simple artifact of roster quality, pitch mix, or sample composition.

In practical terms, the SOV framework allows the study to move from descriptive observation to a more defensible measure of ballpark influence on contact and pitch behavior.

Park-Adjusted Outcome Metric

04 · Pitch Type Frequency

This notebook focuses on how often different pitch types are thrown in each ballpark and whether those frequencies shift meaningfully across seasons. The central idea is that a stadium may alter the strategic environment enough to change how pitchers choose to attack hitters, even when the league-wide sample remains similar.

By comparing park-level pitch mix against league baseline values, the analysis helps determine whether certain venues are associated with a higher share of fastballs, breaking balls, or other pitch categories. These differences can be especially informative because pitch selection is often an early indicator of how a stadium influences batter-pitcher interaction.

  • Park-by-park pitch mix comparisons
  • Seasonal pitch frequency shifts
  • Evidence of venue-driven changes in pitch selection strategy
Pitch Frequency / Mix Analysis

05 · Swing Outcome by Pitch

This section evaluates whether the same pitch type produces different swing outcomes depending on the ballpark. Instead of looking only at total league averages, the analysis shifts to the park level and asks whether a particular pitch behaves differently in one stadium than in another.

The notebook likely reveals whether some parks are associated with a greater share of successful swings, weak contact, or contact that falls into a more favorable outcome bucket. This is a key step because pitch type usage alone does not explain park effect; the important question is how those pitches are actually performing under different environmental conditions.

The experiment is designed to connect strategy and outcome by linking pitch category to the type of swing result it generates within a given stadium context.

Pitch-to-Outcome Comparison

06 · Contact Quality by Pitch

This notebook deepens the analysis by moving beyond simple outcomes to measure contact quality. Rather than focusing solely on whether a swing produced a hit or an out, it examines the quality of contact associated with each pitch type. This matters because a park can influence not only the result of a pitch, but also the underlying quality of underlying contact events.

The analysis seeks to determine which stadiums appear to favor high-quality contact on specific pitch types, and which ones suppress it. This is an important distinction because a park that is merely favorable for outcomes may not necessarily create superior contact quality, and vice versa.

  • Park-level variation in quality of contact
  • Pitch-specific contact-performance gradients
  • Interaction between stadium environment and contact quality
Contact Quality Metrics / Outcome Quality

07 · Park Clustering

The final notebook aggregates the earlier findings into a broader park-level comparison. Rather than treating each stadium independently, it groups venues by similarities in their pitch mix, swing outcomes, and contact-quality patterns. This is where the project begins to speak in terms of ballpark environments rather than isolated anomalies.

The clustering analysis helps reveal whether certain parks behave similarly, whether ballpark effects are stable over time, and whether the overall stadium landscape can be segmented into low-offense, neutral, and high-offense conditions. These groupings provide a more meaningful way to interpret the underlying structure of the data across the league.

In sum, the final notebook connects the project’s descriptive and inferential work into a single environmental perspective on MLB ballpark differences.

Key Interpretation

Taken together, the notebooks trace a structured pipeline from raw event data to park-adjusted evidence on how MLB environments influence pitch strategy and contact quality. The report is designed to show that ballpark effects are not merely descriptive; they can be modeled and compared systematically across stadiums and seasons.
