MLB Ballpark Pitch Type Analysis

Pitch Type, Swing Outcome, and Contact Quality Across Parks

Data Window: 2023 through 2025
Parks: 29 MLB ballparks (Athletics excluded)
Sample: 174,000 swings total (2,000 per park per season, pooled across 3 years)
Core Question: Do park environments shape pitch mix and contact quality?

This write-up follows the notebook sequence for the project: ingesting Statcast swings, setting league baselines, building Swing Output Value, comparing park-level pitch and swing patterns, and clustering parks into data-driven archetypes.

Project Overview

Research Question

Which aspects of a ballpark environment matter most: what pitch types get thrown, what happens on the swing, or how good the contact is when the ball is put in play?

3. Swing Output Value Construction

Notebook 03

SOV is the project's contact-quality scale: it scores balls in play by proximity to an elite-contact zone in EV/LA space, then ranks and rescales those scores to a common interval.

Elite Contact Target Definition

Parameter Value Rationale
Exit Velocity Target 105 mph Well into Statcast barrel territory
Launch Angle Target 28° Center of home-run producing sweet spot
EV Scale Factor 14.85 mph (sample stdev) Normalizes EV to equal footing with LA
LA Scale Factor 28.54° (sample stdev) Normalizes LA to equal footing with EV

Scoring Methodology

  • 2D Gaussian Proximity: Every in-play observation scored based on distance to the 105 mph / 28° target
  • Empirical Percentile Ranking: Raw scores converted to percentile rank (0 to 1) across all 63,625 in-play observations
  • Linear Mapping to [-1, +1]: Percentile converted to SOV scale using formula: SOV = 2p - 1

SOV Interpretation

SOV Value Percentile Contact Profile
+1.0 100th Elite contact (105 mph / 28°)
0.0 50th Median ball in play
-1.0 0th Weakest contact in sample

Key Insight: SOV is uniform by construction across all swings. The analytical power emerges in park-level deviations from that uniform baseline — which parks produce higher or lower SOV scores than league average?

4. Pitch Type Frequency

Notebook 04

This layer asks whether parks change the mix of pitches being thrown, first at the broad category level and then at the pitch-code level for the most distinctive parks.

Layer 1 Finding: 23 of 29 Parks Deviate Significantly

Chi-square goodness-of-fit test against league baseline: 23 of 29 parks (79%) show statistically significant pitch-type deviations (p < 0.05). Only 6 parks are indistinguishable from league average: BOS, SEA, AZ, CIN, SD, PHI.

Focus Parks — Layer 2 Analysis (Pitch Codes)

Five parks with the largest chi-square magnitudes examined at individual pitch level:

Milwaukee Brewers (American Family Field)
Chi-square: 95.99 — Largest deviation in sample

Fastball +6.26 pp above league average. Level 2 reveals the story: Sinker +3.63 pp and Cutter +3.35 pp. Milwaukee throws hard and heavy — emphasizing late-movement fastballs known to generate weak contact, while slider usage sits below average. Organizational velocity identity.

St. Louis Cardinals (Busch Stadium)
Chi-square: 60.21 — Second largest

FF → SI Substitution Pattern. Four-seam fastballs actively replaced by sinkers: FF -3.71 pp, SI +5.25 pp. Ground-ball philosophy at Busch Stadium — a deliberate pitch recipe not a simple volume shift.

Atlanta Braves (Truist Park)
Chi-square: 54.48

Slider +4.94 pp above league average — most spin-heavy attack profile among focus parks. Truist Park pitchers attacking with breaking balls above all else. Stark contrast to Milwaukee's ground-ball identity.

Pittsburgh Pirates (PNC Park)
Chi-square: 43.53

CH → SL Substitution. Changeup usage down 2.29 pp, slider up 2.50 pp. Breaking-ball preferred over offspeed at PNC.

Baltimore Orioles (Camden Yards)
Chi-square: 41.08

SL → FC Substitution. Slider down 3.40 pp, cutter up 2.54 pp. Pitchers staying hard with late movement rather than spinning. (Note: BAL flagged for elevated left-handed batter composition — handedness component possible.)

Key Insight

The most compelling signal is substitution patterns, not volume increases. These parks show distinct pitching recipes: ground balls vs. spin, late movement vs. breaking, hard fastballs vs. soft offspeed. They are not parks throwing more of everything — they are parks with deliberate, organized pitching approaches.

5. Swing Outcomes by Pitch

Notebook 05

The second layer checks whether parks alter what happens after the swing: whiffs, fouls, and balls in play, overall and by pitch type. This answers: Do Milwaukee's sinkers produce different whiff rates than league average? Does Atlanta's slider dominance affect foul-ball frequency?

Analysis Approach

  • Overall outcome rates: Compare each park against the league baseline for swing outcomes (25.5% whiff, 37.7% foul, 36.8% in-play)
  • Pitch-specific effects: See which park and pitch combinations move the needle most on outcome rates

6. Contact Quality by Pitch

Notebook 06

This is the payoff notebook: it brings SOV back in, first to compare park-level mean contact quality overall and then by pitch type with confidence intervals. When batters do make contact in Milwaukee or Atlanta, is the quality systematically different?

Key Steps

  • League reference by pitch type: Set the benchmark SOV for each pitch category before comparing parks to one another
  • Mean SOV by park: Identify which environments suppress or improve contact quality
  • Statistical filtering: Keep the interpretation conservative by pairing intervals with significance checks

7. Park Clustering

Notebook 07

The final notebook folds the earlier layers together into a feature matrix and uses clustering to turn park profiles into broader environment archetypes. Emerge park types: Do similar pitching environments produce similar contact outcomes?

Analysis Approach

  • Feature engineering: Combine pitch mix, swing outcomes, and contact-quality signals into one feature matrix
  • Clustering and labeling: Use diagnostics to choose a cluster count and translate clusters into readable names

Closing Takeaways

The project's storyline is cumulative: parks vary most clearly in what gets thrown (79% show significant deviation), somewhat in how swings resolve, and most selectively in contact quality. The clustering step then turns those differences into a compact set of park archetypes.
